It's Movember, the month many men grow a moustache and, more usefully, finally book the check-up they have been putting off. If you are one of the many men who have had more than one prostate MRI, this post is about one practical thing: **making sure every earlier MRI reaches the radiologist reading your next one.**

## Why a prostate MRI follow-up is a comparison

Men often have a prostate MRI more than once: after a raised PSA test, before or after a biopsy, on a monitoring plan such as active surveillance, or after treatment. Each time, the radiologist is answering two questions:

- **What does the prostate look like today?**
- **What has changed since the last MRI?**

The second question needs the earlier images. A spot that looks the same as two years ago, one that has grown, and one that is new tell three very different stories, and only side-by-side images can show which one you have.

This comparison matters enough that a European expert panel published recommendations, called **PRECISE** (Prostate Cancer Radiological Estimation of Change in Sequential Evaluation), on how radiologists should report change across a series of prostate MRIs in men on active surveillance ([Moore et al., *European Urology*, 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27349615/)). The whole idea assumes one thing: that the earlier scans are available.

What any change means for you is a conversation for your urologist and your radiologist. This post only helps you get the images to them.

## Where your earlier prostate MRIs usually are

If your scans were all done at one hospital, the radiologist may already have them. But many men move between a local imaging center, a hospital for the biopsy, and a specialist center for a second opinion. Earlier MRIs may be:

- **On CDs or DVDs** you received after each scan.
- **In another facility's archive**, which you can ask them to copy for you. Ask for the images, not only the written report.
- **On a USB drive or download link**, which some centers now provide.

Ask early. Copies can take days, so request them a week or two before your appointment.

## Step 1: Check what is on each disc

You don't need to look at the images. You just need to confirm the files are there.

- **On a Mac:** insert the disc (many Macs need an external USB disc drive). It appears on the desktop or in the Finder sidebar. Double-click it to open it as a folder, and ignore any program that tries to start.
- **On Windows:** insert the disc. If a window asks what to do, choose **Open folder to view files**. If nothing happens, open **File Explorer** and click the disc drive (often D: or E:).

Look for a file called **DICOMDIR** or a folder named **DICOM** or **IMAGES**. If you see them, your MRI is there, even if the viewer on the disc won't start. ([What to do when a hospital CD won't open on a Mac](/blog/open-hospital-cd-no-viewer-mac/).)

## Step 2: Put every MRI on one USB

Several discs from several places means the new center has to load each one separately, and some may not load at all. One organized USB is easier for everyone.

## What to bring to the appointment

- The **USB** with your earlier MRIs, plus the **original discs** as a backup.
- Your **radiology and biopsy reports**.
- A short **list of past scans and tests** with dates and places, so nobody has to guess what is missing.

Mention at check-in that you brought previous MRIs, so they can be loaded before the radiologist reads your new one. If the center prefers to request them directly from the other facility, follow their process.

## FAQ

**Why does my radiologist want my previous prostate MRI?**
Because a prostate MRI follow-up is a comparison. The radiologist looks at what the prostate looks like today and at what has changed since earlier scans. That second part needs the earlier images.

**Is the written report from my last MRI enough?**
Ask your center, but the images are usually what makes a side-by-side comparison possible. When you request copies, ask for the images as well as the report.

**My old MRI disc won't open. Is it broken?**
Often not. The viewer program on the disc is what usually fails, not the images. Open the disc as a folder and look for a DICOMDIR file. MIA Toolkit can copy the studies onto one USB.

**Is it safe to keep my scans on a USB?**
MIA Toolkit runs offline, with no account and no cloud, so the images stay on your computer and the USB you create. Keep the USB somewhere safe, as you would any medical record.
